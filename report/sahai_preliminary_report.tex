\begin{abstract}
A language model that solves programming problems is not thereby able to teach
them: the fastest way to make a student's next submission correct is to supply
the answer, which is the one thing a tutor must not do. We train a tutor by
critic-free reinforcement learning against a simulated, knowledge-traced
student, rewarding the student's execution-verified success and penalising
disclosure of the solution, using no human tutoring transcripts. Across twenty
runs the tutor never improved the student's held-out solve rate. We measure the
student's response to six graded levels of help, from a guiding question
through a structural skeleton to the reference solution itself, every hint
constructed programmatically so that no tutor model is involved. On 60
problems, and again on 137 selected for multi-step structure, no level short of
complete disclosure beats offering no help at all. On the
harder set, naming the approach in one sentence and showing a worked solution to
an analogous problem each reduce solve rate by about ten points, while the
reference solution raises it by forty-two. This invalidates the evaluation
rather than only our system: a tutor scored against a simulated learner is
scored by something harmed by partial, non-disclosing hints and helped only by
the answer, which penalises precisely the behaviour pedagogy requires. Three further results
support this. The trained adapter moved two tenths of one percent from its
initialisation; an untrained larger model, prompted plainly, beats it on solve
rate, disclosure and pedagogy at once; and six instrumentation defects
depressed every number this project previously published.
\end{abstract}

Intelligent tutoring systems, knowledge tracing, large language models,

%=======================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%=======================================================================

A language model that can solve a programming problem is not, by
construction, a model that can \emph{teach} it. The two behaviours are in
direct tension: the fastest way to make a learner's next submission correct
is to hand them the answer, which is precisely what tutoring exists to
avoid.

SAHAi treats this as a reinforcement learning problem. A tutor policy
$\pi_\theta$ holds a multi-turn dialogue with a \emph{simulated} student;
the student then attempts the problem; the attempt is executed against unit
tests; and the tutor is rewarded for the student's verified success while
being penalised for disclosing the solution. No corpus of human tutoring
dialogues is used anywhere. This follows the source
review~\cite{rev-pedrl}, which objects to supervised fine-tuning on
teacher--student transcripts on the grounds that few high-quality tutoring
corpora exist publicly.

This report is preliminary, and we state its limits at the outset. Seven
training runs have completed. Held-out solve rate has never left the
$6$--$18\%$ band, and no run-to-run difference in that band is resolvable
at the evaluation set size used. Two things are established. The first is a set of reproducible results
about how reward design behaves under a criticless estimator, each tied to
a measurement rather than to an opinion. The second is a working
interactive system built around the policy.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt]
\item A working, execution-grounded training pipeline for pedagogical RL
      against a simulated learner (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}), and a deployed
      interactive system in which the same pedagogy and leakage
      definitions that are optimised are also the ones served
      (Sec.~\ref{sec:interactive}).
\item A structural incompatibility result: a conversation-level
      \emph{binary} pedagogy reward, as specified for actor--critic
      optimisation, yields exactly zero gradient under group-relative
      advantage estimation (Sec.~\ref{sec:binary}).
\item A quantified reward-channel asymmetry: the terms that are
      deterministic functions of the tutor's own text carry roughly five
      times the within-group variance of the outcome term the system
      exists to optimise, which explains why six successive corrections
      did not move generalisation (Sec.~\ref{sec:channel}).
\item A statistical-power result on our own protocol: the held-out set
      used for every run-to-run comparison to date cannot resolve the
      differences it was used to judge (Sec.~\ref{sec:threats}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Group-relative optimisation}

Policy-gradient methods optimise
$J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[R(\tau)]$. PPO~\cite{ppo}
stabilises this with a clipped importance ratio against a learned value
baseline. Group Relative Policy Optimization~\cite{grpo} removes the value
network: for a group of $G$ trajectories sampled on the same prompt, the
advantage of trajectory $i$ is the within-group $z$-score
\begin{equation}
A_i=\frac{r_i-\mu_{\text{group}}}{\sigma_{\text{group}}+\epsilon}.
\label{eq:adv}
\end{equation}
This halves optimiser memory and removes value-function tuning, at a cost
central to this report: \textbf{within-group reward variance is the entire
learning signal}. A group whose members score identically contributes zero
gradient, a property PPO's learned baseline does not share. Without the
importance ratio, \eqref{eq:adv} reduces to REINFORCE~\cite{reinforce} with
a $z$-scored baseline.

\subsection{Rollout and the simulated student}

For each sampled problem, $G$ dialogues are generated (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).
The student opens with a scripted in-persona utterance; tutor and student
then alternate until a termination phrase or a turn cap. Only tokens
emitted by the tutor enter the loss, via an assistant-span mask; this is
why the student's output may be post-processed and the tutor's may not.

The student is a frozen, 4-bit-quantised instruction model~\cite{qwen}
carrying a persona~\cite{rev-simstudents}: an ability level, per-skill
misconceptions injected into its prompt, and a code-mixing register
(Hinglish, Tanglish or English). It is paired with a Bayesian Knowledge
Tracing~\cite{bkt} model maintaining a per-skill mastery posterior, updated
after every rollout. Ability is obtained from the tracer, never by asking a
model to judge itself~\cite{rev-learnermodel}. Deep Knowledge
Tracing~\cite{dkt} was rejected on data scale: it requires interaction logs
a synthetic student does not produce, whereas BKT is usable from a single
observation and yields a named-skill value that both the ability baseline
and the curriculum sampler require.

\subsection{Reward}

The conversation-level reward is
\begin{equation}
r_{\mathrm{SAHAI}}=\bigl(r_{\mathrm{sol}}-\alpha\bigr)
+\bigl(r_{\mathrm{ped}}-1\bigr)\lambda-\gamma L,
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
with a hard penalty $r_{\mathrm{SAHAI}}=-\lambda$ when
$r_{\mathrm{ped}}=0$. The terms are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt,parsep=0pt]
\item $r_{\mathrm{sol}}$, the post-dialogue solve rate, obtained by
      executing the student's attempt in a sandbox against the problem's
      unit tests.
\item $\alpha$, the tracer's ability baseline. Subtracting it denies
      the tutor credit for a student who could already solve the problem.
\item $r_{\mathrm{ped}}$, a graded rule-based judge averaging three
      checks, all of the form ``did the tutor give the answer away''.
\item $L\in[0,1]$, the leakage term. It combines rare-token overlap
      between tutor text and the reference solution, with
      problem-statement tokens subtracted, plus an execution check under
      which tutor-written code that passes the problem's own tests scores
      $L{=}1$ regardless of lexical similarity.
\end{itemize}

Advantages follow \eqref{eq:adv} over groups sharing a problem. The loss
adds a KL penalty to a frozen reference policy, realised as the same
weights with adapters disabled. Problems are drawn by a curriculum sampler
operating on the tracer's mastery state, following the Zone of Proximal
Development~\cite{vygotsky}.

\section{The Interactive System}
\label{sec:interactive}

An untrained policy is not a usable tutor, so the other half of SAHAi is six
containerised services. We describe them briefly because two of their
constraints shaped the training half, and because this half is finished where
the training half is not.

The training and serving halves were initially one codebase, and they have
different shapes: training drives a model in a batch loop and discards its
state, while serving answers one turn at a time and must survive restarts.
Splitting the dialogue engine into a batch runner and a state machine over a
persisted transcript removed that conflation, with scoring kept in a shared
library so that pedagogy and leakage cannot diverge between the two.

Scoring and execution are separated by construction. Learner code runs only in
an executor container with no network egress, a non-root user, a read-only
filesystem, a fresh interpreter per test case, and process and memory limits;
the verdict is read from a sentinel line so a candidate program cannot forge
it. The learner interface provides a workspace with a code editor, a progress
view, a graded placement test, and speech input for Indic languages, with
per-skill mastery persisted in an append-only observation log.

No cohort has used any of it, so nothing here is evidence that the interface,
the placement seeding or the curriculum improves learning.

the mean over those problems. Leakage at evaluation is fitted on the
\emph{training} corpus, so that ``rare token'' means the same thing in both
phases. All values below are taken from the artifacts of a single completed
run, inspected directly.

\subsection{Training dynamics}

Every aggregate moves in the intended direction
(Table~\ref{tab:epochs}). Mean reward rises from
$-0.576$ to $+0.071$ and first becomes positive at epoch 5. Pedagogy rises
from $0.802$ to $0.988$ while leakage falls from $0.328$ to $0.083$. The
correlation between epoch and $r_{\mathrm{sol}}$ reaches $+0.65$, higher
than in any previous run, where it was $+0.48$, $+0.54$ and $+0.01$.

KL divergence to the frozen reference ends at $0.024$, having risen
monotonically. This is roughly five times the $0.005$ reached by an earlier
20-epoch run, and it is the one unambiguous confirmation that raising the
learning rate from $2\times10^{-5}$ to $1\times10^{-4}$ did what it was
meant to do. The policy now measurably moves away from the base model.
Even so should not be read as evidence of learning
to teach, for reasons developed in Sec.~\ref{sec:channel}.

\subsection{The controlled comparison}
\label{sec:controlled}

Every evaluation before this one scored a single arm against a number
remembered from an earlier run, at $n{=}20$, unpaired. At that size a
two-proportion test cannot detect an improvement below roughly 40 points, and
no difference this project reported was that large. We therefore re-ran with
every arm on the same problems in the same order, compared by McNemar's exact
test on the discordant problems.

Two framings of the student's final attempt are reported. Under \emph{full} the
transcript is replayed with the student's own turns in the model's assistant
history. Under \emph{hints} only the tutor's turns reach the attempt. The two
are compared by replaying one fixed set of saved transcripts twice, so the
framing is the only thing that varies.

Table~\ref{tab:threearm} gives the arms and Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar} the tests.
Three readings follow.

\textbf{The harm we reported was ours.} Under the full framing a tutored
student solves $0.183$ against $0.367$ untutored, $p{=}0.0074$, which is the
result this project reported in five forms. Replaying the identical transcripts
without the student's own replies lifts the same arm to $0.350$, $p{=}1.0000$.
No tutoring changed, only whether the student had its own words in front of it.
The mechanism is unknown: the explanation we first gave, that the model
conditions on its own recorded confusion, fails its own prediction, and so does
the alternative that the assistant role is responsible.

\textbf{Training is inert, and the one result suggesting otherwise was the
artefact again.} Under the corrected framing both adapters score $0.350$, five
discordant each way. Under the broken framing the trained adapter won at
$p{=}0.0352$, which measured its partial immunity to our instrument rather than
better teaching.

\textbf{The prize is larger than we stated.} Disclosure reaches $0.783$ rather
than the $0.633$ previously reported, two defects of Sec.~\ref{sec:instrument}
accounting for the difference, so the headroom is $0.417$ against a protocol
resolving about $0.15$.

A caveat on precision. The attempt is decoded greedily, so replaying a saved
transcript should reproduce it exactly; it reproduces 57 of 60 in both arms.
The residual is nondeterminism in GPU reductions and sets a floor of about
three problems per arm, which the untrained arm's framing effect clears and the
trained arm's does not.

\subsection{Six instrumentation defects}
\label{sec:instrument}

Each was found by asking why a result looked as it did, not by a test failing,
and each changed numbers we had published.

\textbf{The verifier failed correct code.} The student's output passes through a
JSON round trip and the expected value, parsed from the problem's assertion,
does not. Comparing them directly failed 9 of 60 reference solutions against
their own tests, so the achievable maximum was never $1.0$.

\textbf{The seed was never applied}, then applied too late. It was read nowhere
in the training path, so identical configurations drew different problems before
any gradient step and a two-arm experiment was not paired. Once fixed it still
ran after the adapter's random initialisation, so runs diverged from the first
update.

\textbf{The entry point was the wrong function.} Each problem's callable was
taken from the first function the reference defines, while the name the tests
call was parsed from the assertion and discarded. These differ whenever a
reference opens with a helper. The verifier then executed the helper and
compared it against the entry point's expected value, and the same field reaches
the student's prompt as the signature to implement. Five of 60 held-out
problems were unwinnable in every arm and sat in every denominator we reported.

\textbf{Correct code was discarded over its name.} The extraction step sought
the literal expected definition and, failing, replaced the reply with a stub
that fails by construction. Every mismatch observed was styling, the dataset
using mixed case where the student writes snake case; three otherwise-correct
solutions scored zero.

\textbf{The transcript was replayed harmfully}, which Sec.~\ref{sec:controlled}
reports, and \textbf{the adapter was never weighed}, which
Sec.~\ref{sec:nomove} reports.

The entry-point and extraction fixes together raise the disclosure ceiling from
$0.633$ to $0.783$.

\textbf{The stopping criterion is the probe, not the loss and not the reward.}
The group-relative surrogate is near zero at every episode by construction,
because advantages are $z$-scored within their group and therefore sum to zero;
measured policy loss across this run ran $0.0008$ to $0.0042$ and gives no
descending curve to converge. Stopping on reward would be worse than useless:
an earlier run took held-out solve from $16.3\%$ to $6.2\%$ while its mean
reward reached its first positive value, so reward rising is a documented
failure mode of this project rather than a hypothetical one.

The probe therefore drives both stopping and checkpoint selection. It rose
across the first three measurements and fell at the fourth, so the selected
model is episode 5 and not the final episode. Two earlier seeded runs peaked
sooner still, at episodes 1 and 3, which is why the budget was eight rather
than a larger number.

\textbf{Two cautions about Table~\ref{tab:episodes}.} The probe is 20 problems,
so each step of $0.05$ is one problem and the series $0.150, 0.250, 0.350,
0.250$ is three, five, seven and five problems solved. That is consistent with
noise around $0.25$, and we initially over-read the first three points as a
trend before the fourth arrived. Separately, episode 7's apparent jump in
rollout solve rate is a property of the problems drawn rather than of the
policy: across episodes, the correlation between solve rate and the syntactic
size of the problems drawn is $-0.623$, against $+0.434$ for episode number,
and episode 7 drew the smallest problems of the run. Rollout solve rate is not
a learning signal here, and the probe falling in the same episode confirms it.

\subsection{The policy never moved}
\label{sec:nomove}

its up-projection $B$ to \emph{exactly} zero, so the adapter contributes
nothing at step 0 whatever $A$ holds, and the entire effect of training is
visible in how far $B$ travelled from zero.

Table~\ref{tab:movement} gives the magnitudes. With $\alpha/r = 2$ at rank 8 this is an effective weight perturbation near
$5\times10^{-5}$ against base weights of order $0.02$, about $0.2\%$. The

run reached $0.0301$ in the same column at the same learning rate.

\textbf{The surrogate is why.} The advantage in \eqref{eq:adv} is a
within-group $z$-score, so advantages sum to zero and at the first
accumulation step the importance ratio is exactly one. Measured policy loss
across this run ran $0.0008$ to $0.0042$. That loss is additionally a mean over
tokens rather than a sum, dividing the gradient by sequence length again, and
it is opposed by a KL penalty 50 times stronger than the one used by the work
this method follows. Small loss, twice normalised, actively pulled back.

\textbf{What this does to every other result here.} The reward terms in
Sec.~\ref{sec:hack} were debugged, graded, re-weighted and extended across
twenty runs; the measurement was found wrong in four separate ways and
corrected; and throughout all of it the policy being optimised stayed within
$0.2\%$ of its initialisation. Every null this paper reports is consistent with
a tutor that never changed. We report it as the simplest available explanation
and prefer it to any account involving the reward.

The check costs one minute from a saved adapter and we had never run it. It
should precede any claim about reward design.

\subsection{Only the complete answer helps}
\label{sec:ladder}

Two points were known on this bank and nothing between them: $0.433$ with no
dialogue, $0.783$ with the solution disclosed. Every experiment above lives in
that gap, and none of them asked whether the gap is reachable by anything short
of disclosure.

We measure six levels of hint concreteness. Every hint is constructed
programmatically from the reference solution, so no tutor model is involved and
no policy quality can confound the result. This measures the task and the

splits pooled, keeping the 137 of 558 references with at least four statements
in the function body and at least one loop or conditional, averaging $11.9$
lines and $2.9$ control-flow nodes. Pooling is sound because no trained model
is involved.

The learner borrows the example's function name on 5 problems against 4

no code and harms by exactly as much as the worked example, $0.234$ against

mechanism.

What the code shows instead is two different routes of comparable size, neither
significant alone. Naming the approach moves the learner's output \emph{away}
from the correct solution, $0.359 \rightarrow 0.319$, without moving it toward
anything. The worked example leaves it as close to correct but inflates it,
$76.2 \rightarrow 84.4$ syntax-tree nodes, and on the problems it loses the
learner writes more code than unaided in 17 of 24 cases ($p{=}0.064$).

\textbf{Partial help is not inert, and the averages hide that.} The
one-sentence rung fixes 6 problems the learner cannot do alone and breaks 20;
the worked example fixes 10 and breaks 24. Help works, and less often than it
backfires, which is a different and more uncomfortable claim than help being
useless.

\textbf{We state the mechanism as unidentified.} Three explanations were
offered in this paper for one effect: that our prompt forbade the register that
works, which a think-aloud study of twelve novice programmers across four hint
levels gave us reason to expect~\cite{hintlevels}; that short reference
solutions made structural hints vacuous; and that the learner copies worked
examples. Each was falsified by a measurement run to
test it. The effect is firm across two banks and 197 problems; settling the
mechanism needs one hint property varied at a time at a sample size resolving
$0.05$, roughly 800 problems per rung against our 137.

\textbf{This generalises past our system, and it is the paper's most useful
claim.} A tutor evaluated against a simulated learner is scored by something
harmed by partial, non-disclosing hints and helped only by the answer. That
penalises precisely the behaviour pedagogy requires, so any reward built on such
a learner pushes a tutor toward disclosure or toward silence. Every reward here
inherited it, as does any evaluation that scores tutors against a
language-model learner. That line of work rests on the assumption that a
simulated student responds to teaching as a student
would~\cite{rev-simstudents}, and we are not aware of a paper that tests it.
Our two ladders are such a test, and the assumption does not hold for a 1.5B
learner on program synthesis. A randomised trial in high-school mathematics is
adjacent but does not straightforwardly support us: unguarded access to a
general assistant improved supported practice and reduced subsequent unaided
performance by $17\%$ against students who never had access, and that harm was
\emph{largely removed} by a guardrailed tutor~\cite{bastani}. Our hints are
guardrailed by construction, since the ladder's non-disclosing rungs withhold
the solution, and they still failed. So the two results share a direction
without sharing a mechanism, and theirs suggests a remedy that ours has already
excluded.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A binary pedagogy reward yields exactly zero gradient}
\label{sec:binary}

The source review~\cite{rev-pedrl} specifies
$r_{\mathrm{ped}}=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}[J_m=\text{accept}]$. The first
run logged a loss of exactly zero at epoch 0. Every rollout failed at least
one rubric, so $r_{\mathrm{ped}}{=}0$ for all $G$; the hard penalty mapped
all of them to $r{=}-\lambda$; with identical rewards
$\sigma_{\text{group}}{=}0$ in \eqref{eq:adv}; every advantage became zero.

The failure is structural rather than incidental. Under PPO a binary
conversation-level reward remains viable, because a learned value baseline
tolerates constant returns. A critic-free group-relative estimator has no
such fallback, since within-group variance \emph{is} the signal, and the
smaller $G$ is the more certain the collapse becomes. Scoring the fraction of checks passed rather
than their conjunction raised reward spread from $0.000$ to $1.317$ and
epoch-0 loss from $0.0000$ to $0.0562$. The hard penalty was retained; it
now fires only when a dialogue fails every check.

\subsection{Reward hacking and measurement artifacts}
\label{sec:hack}

Three further defects were found by direct measurement.

\textbf{Length bias.} Four of five pedagogy checks scanned every tutor turn
and failed the whole dialogue on any single violation, so the probability
of passing decayed with turn count regardless of quality. Over 304
rollouts, mean $r_{\mathrm{ped}}$ by tutor-turn count was
$0.720/0.639/0.537/0.453$ for one to four turns: perfectly monotonic, and
$33.6\%$ of dialogues ended after a single tutor turn with the run's best
rewards. Scoring those checks per-turn reduced the spread across dialogue
lengths from $0.267$ to $0.044$.

\textbf{Gaming a behavioural incentive.} Converting an inert ``asks
questions'' threshold into a per-turn fraction raised training
$r_{\mathrm{ped}}$ to $0.992$ and produced the first positive mean training
reward, while held-out solve rate fell to $6.2\%$. A seven-word contentless
question scores $1.00$ on every check, and under a fraction each
un-questioned teaching turn costs reward directly. An attempted content
gate failed on inspection: gamed turns held 3--12 content words and genuine
questions 4--13. The incentive was reverted and pedagogy narrowed to three
checks under an explicit rule. \emph{Keep a check when satisfying the rule
and achieving the goal are the same act; drop it when the rule is a proxy
satisfiable without the goal.} The cost should be stated plainly:
nothing in the reward now rewards Socratic behaviour, and only a fall in
solve rate would register a drift back to lecturing.

\textbf{Two terms that did not measure what they named.} Leakage compared
tutor text against the \emph{reference} solution, so a tutor that wrote a
complete working solution using a different algorithm scored $0.000$: the
term was detecting reuse of one implementation, not disclosure of the
answer. It now scores $1.000$ for that case, which is why the definition in
Sec.~\ref{sec:method} carries an execution check. Separately, four of the
five original pedagogy checks were negative conditions, so a dialogue with
no tutor turns at all passed them vacuously and scored $0.8$. In training,
a rollout in which the tutor said nothing would have been rewarded above
average.

\textbf{A curriculum band that empties permanently.} The band tested mean
skill mastery against $[0.3,0.7]$ and ignored the difficulty argument it
was passed. With the tracer prior equal to the lower bound, the band in
effect meant ``skills never attempted''; and because the tracer has no
forgetting transition, a failed skill converges to $\approx0.109$ within
three observations. The band therefore holds the whole bank at epoch 0 and
is empty permanently once every skill has been touched. The reported run
logged three top-up warnings across ten epochs, with band sizes of 2 and 0
against a required 4.

\subsection{Only half the reward can teach}
\label{sec:channel}

Equation~\eqref{eq:reward} contains two kinds of term. Pedagogy and leakage are
\textbf{deterministic} functions of the tutor's own text: identical text scores
identically and the policy controls them outright. The outcome term is a
\textbf{sampled estimate} of whether a different, frozen model writes passing
code afterwards, which the tutor influences only weakly and indirectly.

Across the last three ten-episode runs the correlation between episode and
pedagogy was $+0.80/+0.99/+0.64$ and between episode and leakage
$-0.64/-0.80/-0.48$, while between episode and the outcome term it was
$+0.48/+0.54/+0.01$. On mean between-group variance, which is the only variance
that becomes gradient under~\eqref{eq:adv}, pedagogy measured $0.0482$ against
$0.0098$ for the outcome: roughly five times the usable signal on the term the
project exists to optimise, with 22 of 24 rollouts scoring the outcome exactly
zero.

That ratio reversed under the change made to fix it. With greedy decoding and
partial test-case credit the outcome term measures $0.0481$ and pedagogy
$0.0150$, and held-out solve still does not move. Two qualifications keep this
from being a clean reversal: $76\%$ of rollouts still score exactly zero, and
$197$ of $198$ problems carry exactly three assertions, so the continuous
outcome term has four levels. Gradient starvation was real and worth removing.
It was not what prevented learning, and we report both measurements rather than
the one that suits the argument.

A policy optimises whatever part of its reward it can control, which is the
pattern in Sec.~\ref{sec:results}: gains followed repairs to the controllable
channel, the one behavioural incentive added was gamed within a run, and
teaching that causes a student to solve was never trained for want of a usable
gradient.

\subsection{What the transcripts show}
\label{sec:transcripts}

The aggregate result says tutoring does not help; the transcripts say why,
and the answer is not that the tutor is vague. It is confidently and
specifically wrong. Asked about the volume of a triangular prism it opens with
Heron's formula, and the student commits to a signature the problem does not
want. Shown a student's broken string-splitting function it replies
``Great job revising your function! Your updated version looks good now''
about code that fails its tests.

Harm scales with how much is said. Across 60 dialogues,
$\mathrm{corr}(\text{tutor characters},\text{solved})=-0.17$. On the problems
where tutoring turned a solved problem unsolved the tutor produced $1443$
characters on average; where tutoring rescued a problem, $450$. Split into
terciles by tutor verbosity, solve rate runs $0.350$, $0.200$, $0.200$, and
the quietest third \emph{exceeds} the $0.317$ untutored baseline measured in
the same run. Tutoring is non-harmful only when the tutor says almost nothing.

Nothing in the reward asks whether what the tutor says is true. The three
pedagogy checks are all negative: no dangling promises, no code blocks, no
solution patterns. Leakage asks only whether the answer escaped. A tutor
that confidently recommends an inapplicable technique satisfies every one of
them and scores $r_{\mathrm{ped}}{=}1.000$.

\subsection{Adding a correctness term does not help}
\label{sec:correctness}

We added the missing term. Agreement between the tutor's technical claims and
the problem's reference solution is scored by a 7B judge in 4-bit, and the
judge was validated before any policy trained against it: on $180$ saved
transcripts it ranks dialogues where tutoring rescued a problem $+0.409$ above
those where tutoring destroyed one, where a 1.5B judge scored the harmful ones
\emph{higher} and was discarded for it.

Two arms, identical in seed, problems, epochs, judge and early stopping,
differing only in the weight on that term. Both selected epoch 1 by early
stopping on a validation split disjoint from the test set.

Correctness rises during training whether rewarded or not, as a by-product of

We registered the falsification before the run: if correctness climbed while
held-out solve did not, the term should be removed rather than retuned. It
fails more completely than that, since it did not drive its own metric better
than an arm ignoring it. The coefficient is $0$ in the released configuration.

One design point generalises. Both the correctness term and the judge
originally scored the tutor against the reference solution's \emph{approach},
which punishes a tutor for recommending a different approach that also works.
Of 23 student solutions that pass every test, 11 had a detectable technique on
both sides and 2 used a different one: \texttt{opposite\_Signs} is
$(x \oplus y) < 0$ in the dataset and $x \cdot y < 0$ in a passing solution.
This repeats a mistake already made and already fixed elsewhere in the
system. Leakage was once token overlap against the reference and scored
$0.000$ for a tutor that wrote a complete working solution by a different
algorithm, and was fixed by executing the code instead of comparing it.
Removing the anchoring halves the judge's discrimination, $+0.409$ to $+0.212$,
because the verdicts move from \textsc{neutral} to \textsc{helpful} rather than
out of \textsc{wrong}. We keep the unanchored version and report the cost: a
diagnostic that is wrong about $18\%$ of valid advice is worse than one that is
blurry.

\subsection{A tutor with its student's weights has nothing to teach}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

Tutor and student were the same checkpoint for the entire project. Both are
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, and the configuration records the choice as deliberate:
the student was raised from a smaller model to match the tutor's size after the
smaller one failed to hold a confused persona under reinforcement learning
pressure.

A tutor holding its student's exact weights knows nothing the student does not.
It cannot transfer information, because there is none to transfer. The only
channel left open to it is organisation: decomposition, ordering, and the
timing of hints. Measured, organisation is worth nothing, $0.350$ against
$0.367$ for no dialogue at all.

This is not a reward problem and not an optimisation problem, and it interacts
badly with the method. A policy gradient reweights behaviour the policy already
produces; it cannot synthesise capability the base model lacks. If no rollout
in a group ever contains teaching that would help, there is nothing in the
sample for the estimator to reinforce, whatever the reward says about it.

The oracle arm establishes that the receiving end works. Disclose the solution
and the same student reaches $0.783$, twenty-eight problems gained against
three lost. The channel is open and the student absorbs what arrives on it.
Nothing useful was being sent.

Two routes follow and we have only begun the first. An asymmetry can be
manufactured by weakening the student, which also lowers the untutored baseline
and so creates the headroom this evaluation lacked: $60\%$ of the held-out bank
was previously inert, solved or failed alike by every arm. Or capability can be
imported, by having a stronger model generate tutoring dialogues, filtering
them by whether the student then solved, and fine-tuning the small tutor on
what survives. The second addresses the cause rather than the symptom and is
the direction we would now take.

\subsection{Was reinforcement learning the right tool?}
\label{sec:method-choice}

The case for it was sound: no corpus of human programming-tutoring dialogue
exists at the scale supervised learning needs, and the outcome of tutoring is
cheaply verifiable by execution. Absent demonstrations and present a verifier
is the setting reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards exists for.

The case for a critic-free estimator was also sound, and for a hardware reason
rather than a statistical one, as Sec.~\ref{sec:method} notes. What it costs is
that the learning signal becomes entirely within-group reward variance, and
much of this project's reward engineering was in retrospect an effort to keep
that variance non-zero. By the final run it had succeeded: no group was dead
and the outcome term carried the largest share of usable variance. The policy
still did not move, for the reason in Sec.~\ref{sec:nomove}.

Given what we measure, a simpler objective fits our budget better.
Rejection-sampling fine-tuning trains only on successful rollouts and is
reported competitive with both group-relative and proximal policy
optimisation, with the observation that the former's advantage comes from
discarding prompts whose rollouts all fail rather than from normalising
rewards~\cite{raft}. The decisive property for us is different: an ordinary
cross-entropy objective has a loss of order one rather than $0.002$, so the
policy moves. Its weakness is equally clear, since the 45 usable dialogues in
our own rollouts cover only 15 distinct problems.

We therefore do not conclude that reinforcement learning is wrong for
pedagogical alignment, but that a critic-free policy gradient at this compute
scale produces a gradient too small to move a policy, and that the constraint
which made it attractive is the one that made it ineffective.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{The missing baseline was this project's largest defect, and running it
changed the sign of the conclusion twice.} Every run before this revision
reported a solve rate after tutoring with no control, and the harness had been
written to accept several arms while only ever being given one. A number that
read as weak progress, $17.5\%$ against a previous best of $16.3\%$, became a
deficit against doing nothing, and then became an artefact of the transcript
framing. We regard this as the most transferable result in the paper.

\textbf{The evaluation resolves less than the effects it was asked to detect.}
At $n{=}20$ a bootstrap of the mean has standard deviation $0.068$ at a true
rate of $0.10$, so every run-to-run conclusion in this project's history rests
on about two problems. The held-out set is now 60 and the ladder bank 137,
resolving about $0.15$ and $0.17$ respectively, which is why we report
directions rather than results for the solve column throughout.

\textbf{The reported run predated its own configuration.} An earlier run
executed code preceding the staged corrections, so the method as run was
REINFORCE with a $z$-scored baseline rather than clipped group-relative
optimisation, with a four-sample all-or-nothing outcome term and a
mastery-banded curriculum. Every number in Sec.~\ref{sec:controlled} onward
comes from runs where all corrections are present and logged.

\textbf{The simulator is a model, not a learner.} A policy can in principle
raise reward by exploiting simulator quirks rather than by teaching. The
designed defence, an assistance-corrected effect probe, is implemented and has
never been supplied with data. Separately, all pedagogy checks are syntactic
and leakage is lexical plus execution-based, so a tutor stating an algorithm
entirely in prose scores perfectly on both.

\textbf{No human validation.} Sec.~\ref{sec:interactive} describes function,
not efficacy. No cohort has used the system, so nothing here shows that the
interface, placement seeding, curriculum or turn adaptation improves learning.

\subsection{Threats introduced by this revision}

\textbf{The corrected framing is itself a choice and nothing was trained
against it.} Omitting the student's own replies is not obviously the right
reconstruction of what a tutored learner knows; it is one of three framings we
implemented and the one we adopted scored highest, which is a weak basis for
calling it correct. What we can defend is narrower: the framings disagree by
more than the effect every arm was built to detect, so no conclusion that does
not state its framing is interpretable. Every policy reported here was trained
with the outcome term computed under the framing we now believe distorting.

\textbf{Several defects were found in one audit of one arm.} Two of the six
came from asking why the disclosure arm fails at all, a question not asked for
six runs. We have no reason to believe the audit is complete, and the base rate
it establishes is poor: every assumed measurement we have examined has been
wrong.

\textbf{The ladder's mechanism tests are underpowered.} The analogy rung's
code-similarity contrast is $n{=}137$ with the decisive subgroup at $n{=}24$,
and the two damage routes we can distinguish sit at $p{=}0.064$ and above. The
effect is firm; no mechanism for it is.

\textbf{The correctness experiment is a single pair of runs.} Both arms were
seeded identically and drew the same problems through episode 4, diverging
afterwards because a different reward produces a different curriculum, which is
the treatment propagating rather than a confound but does leave the arms
unmatched late in training. One pair cannot separate a null from an effect
smaller than run-to-run variance, which we have not characterised.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We have described an end-to-end system that trains a programming tutor by
critic-free reinforcement learning against a simulated, knowledge-traced
student with execution-verified supervision, together with a working serving
half described in Sec.~\ref{sec:interactive}.

\textbf{The tutor does not teach better, and we can now say why rather than
only that.} Across twenty runs no configuration improved the simulated
student's held-out solve rate. The explanation is not the reward, which was
debugged, graded, re-weighted and extended; not the optimiser, which moved
every term it could reach; and not the policy, which we measure at $0.2\%$ from
its initialisation. It is that on this task the learner is helped only by being
given the answer. Six graded levels of help across 197 problems show that
nothing short of complete disclosure beats offering nothing, and that two
intermediate levels do significant harm.

\textbf{The transferable result is about evaluation.} A simulated learner
damaged by partial, non-disclosing hints and rescued only by the solution
cannot be used to score a tutor, because the reward it induces points away from
teaching toward either disclosure or silence. That is what every reward here
did, and it is a property of the evaluation rather than of our implementation
of it. The assumption that a language-model student responds to teaching as a
student does is widely relied upon and, as far as we can find, untested; our
two ladders test it directly and it fails for a 1.5B learner on program
synthesis. The check is cheap, needing no training and no tutor model, and we
would want it run before any further tutor is optimised against a simulated
student.

\textbf{What we got wrong.} Every substantive correction here came from a
measurement assumed rather than taken: an untutored arm nobody had run, a seed
applied after the adapter was built, an entry point read from the wrong
function, an extraction step discarding correct code over a capitalised name, a
transcript framing worth more than the effect the protocol could detect, and an
adapter nobody had weighed. We also offered three mechanisms for the central
effect and each was falsified by a measurement run to test it, so we report the
mechanism as unidentified rather than adopting the least damaged of the three.

\textbf{Future work.} Running the ladder across model scales would say whether
the effect belongs to small models or to language-model learners generally, and
costs no training. Settling the mechanism needs one hint property varied at a
time at roughly 800 problems per level. And a tutor intended for deployment
should be compared against a prompted larger model on outcome and disclosure
together, since on our measurements that baseline dominates a trained small
model on both.
